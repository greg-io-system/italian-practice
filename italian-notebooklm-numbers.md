# Italian Numbers: From Counting to Real Conversations

## Why Numbers Matter for Travel

Numbers are the skeleton key of Italian travel. You need them at every turn — reading prices at a market stall, telling the host how many are in your party, buying train tickets, understanding platform announcements, and haggling over souvenirs. This guide drills the numbers themselves, then immediately puts them into the situations you'll face.

## The Core Numbers: 0–20

These are the building blocks. Every number above 20 is just a combination of these.

- 0 — zero (DZEH-roh)
- 1 — uno (OO-noh)
- 2 — due (DOO-eh)
- 3 — tre (TREH)
- 4 — quattro (KWAT-troh)
- 5 — cinque (CHEEN-kweh)
- 6 — sei (SAY)
- 7 — sette (SET-teh)
- 8 — otto (OT-toh)
- 9 — nove (NOH-veh)
- 10 — dieci (dee-EH-chee)
- 11 — undici (OON-dee-chee)
- 12 — dodici (DOH-dee-chee)
- 13 — tredici (TREH-dee-chee)
- 14 — quattordici (kwat-TOR-dee-chee)
- 15 — quindici (KWEEN-dee-chee)
- 16 — sedici (SEH-dee-chee)
- 17 — diciassette (dee-chas-SET-teh)
- 18 — diciotto (dee-CHOT-toh)
- 19 — diciannove (dee-chan-NOH-veh)
- 20 — venti (VEN-tee)

## Tens: 20–100

- 20 — venti
- 30 — trenta (TREN-tah)
- 40 — quaranta (kwah-RAN-tah)
- 50 — cinquanta (cheen-KWAN-tah)
- 60 — sessanta (ses-SAN-tah)
- 70 — settanta (set-TAN-tah)
- 80 — ottanta (ot-TAN-tah)
- 90 — novanta (noh-VAN-tah)
- 100 — cento (CHEN-toh)

## Combining Numbers: The Rule

For 21–99, combine the tens word with the unit. Drop the final vowel of the tens word before "uno" and "otto":
- 21 = ventuno (not ventiuno)
- 28 = ventotto (not ventiotto)
- 33 = trentatré
- 45 = quarantacinque
- 67 = sessantasette

## Hundreds and Beyond (for prices and distances)

- 100 — cento
- 200 — duecento
- 500 — cinquecento
- 1,000 — mille (MEE-leh)
- 2,000 — duemila (doo-eh-MEE-lah)

Note: In Italian, a comma separates decimals and a period separates thousands. So €1.500 means one thousand five hundred euros, and €3,50 means three euros fifty.

## Numbers in Real Situations

### Entering a café or restaurant
- "Quanti siete?" — How many are you? (the host will ask this)
- "Siamo in due." — There are two of us.
- "Siamo in tre." — There are three of us.
- "Un tavolo per quattro, per favore." — A table for four, please.
- "Solo uno." — Just one. (if dining alone)

### Ordering quantities
- "Due caffè, per favore." — Two coffees, please.
- "Tre cornetti." — Three croissants.
- "Una bottiglia d'acqua." — One bottle of water.
- "Due bicchieri di vino rosso." — Two glasses of red wine.

### Understanding prices
- "Quanto costa?" — How much does it cost?
- "Costa cinque euro." — It costs five euros.
- "Sono dodici euro e cinquanta." — That's twelve euros fifty.
- "Quindici euro in tutto." — Fifteen euros total.

### Buying tickets and getting around
- "Due biglietti per Roma, per favore." — Two tickets to Rome, please.
- "A che ora parte?" — What time does it leave?
- "Parte alle tre e mezza." — It leaves at three thirty.
- "Binario sette." — Platform seven.
- "La fermata numero cinque." — Stop number five.

### Telling time (essential for schedules)
- "Che ore sono?" — What time is it?
- "Sono le due." — It's two o'clock.
- "Sono le dieci e un quarto." — It's quarter past ten.
- "A mezzogiorno." — At noon.
- "Alle otto di sera." — At eight in the evening.

## Common Traps and Tips

1. "Uno" changes gender: "un caffè" (masculine) vs "una pizza" (feminine)
2. When counting items, use the appropriate article: "due panini" not "due un panini"
3. Italians often gesture numbers differently — they start counting with the thumb, not the index finger. So "one" is the thumb extended, "two" is thumb plus index finger.
4. For prices, you'll often hear just the number without "euro": "Sono quindici" means "That's fifteen (euros)."
